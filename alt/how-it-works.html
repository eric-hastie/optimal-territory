Fit scoring

How I think about fit

Five rules that decide what gets counted. They are the reason the list looks the way it does, and each one exists because ignoring it produced a board that ranked the wrong thing.

01

Two scores, never one number

Fit answers whether an account should ever be worked and moves on the scale of quarters. Intent answers whether to call this week. Adding them together hides the most interesting quadrant of all: the perfect-fit account that is quiet right now, which is a nurture play, and the poor-fit account that is loud, which is a trap.

02

Score the buying unit, not the company

Your two largest published customers did not buy as companies. One bought for an internal tools team, the other through a subsidiary. A model that reads company headcount rejects both. So size is read as the team that owns review, and a large organisation qualifies through one team with one owner.

03

Never score the criterion you sourced on

If a universe is built by searching for a signal, every account in it has that signal, and scoring it ranks nothing. I learned this the expensive way on an earlier board where a single sourcing criterion accounted for most of every score and half the accounts collapsed onto two values.

04

A blank is not a zero, and the score has to admit it

Absence of evidence gets treated as evidence constantly, and it quietly turns unmeasured accounts into bad ones. Where a missing value scores zero, the row also carries the score it would reach if that gap were filled. The distance between the two tells you whether you are looking at a judgment or a hole.

05

Every number carries the sentence it came from

Each score links back to the text it was read from, because keyword matches lie in specific ways. A payment processor's certificate quoted on a customer's security page is not that customer's certificate. A definition of a standard is not a claim to hold it. Those were caught by reading matches, not by trusting counts.

What this buys

A list where every row can be argued with. If a score looks wrong, the evidence column says where it came from and the coverage marker says how much of it was actually measured, so the conversation is about the account rather than about the model.
