Fit scoring

What the customer set shows, and how it became a score

Seven published case studies, sixteen named customers and five recorded conversations, read for the patterns that repeat. Those patterns are the model.

01

The buying unit is a team, and usually a small one

Five of the seven published case studies are lean engineering teams, most in the eleven to fifty range, and one describes its bottleneck as the availability of the second engineer. The two large logos bought the same way: one for an internal tools team, the other through an engineering subsidiary. So team size is the strongest single predictor, and a large company qualifies through one team with one owner.

02

Every reference describes the same bottleneck

Juniors waiting a full day for a senior in another timezone. Progress freezing when the second engineer was busy. Everything routed through a single security owner. Different companies, different verticals, one mechanic: a required step that only one or two people can perform. That is what the product removes, and it is what the model looks for.

03

The pattern is data sensitivity, not industry

Seven customers span seven unrelated verticals. The attribute that repeats, in four of the seven, is regulated or sensitive data: tax, health, credentialing, financial. That is a property of what the code touches, so it raises a score and leaves selection to the structural terms.

04

Volume grows on a flat team

The clearest customer story is pull request volume rising eight to ten times with no new engineers, because everyone started shipping code, product owners and specialists included. The signal is load per reviewer and the direction it is moving.

05

Turning that into a score

Six weighted terms, summing to a hundred so a score reads as a percentage of achievable fit. Team size carries the most weight at 42, since it is what the evidence supports most strongly. Source control 18, engineering as a share of the company 15, data sensitivity 10, distributed engineering 8, and staff in the event metros 7. Two boosts sit on top and are recorded separately: ten points where an account was sourced as a competitor or structural twin of a named customer, and eight where a live regulatory clock applies.

Fit and intent answer different questions

This score says whether an account is the right shape and it moves on the scale of quarters. A second model measures what a company is doing right now in its own code activity, and that one moves weekly. The lists are built from fit; the sequencing comes from intent.
