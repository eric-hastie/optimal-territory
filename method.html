How the ICP fit score is calculated

The shape of the model

One question, answered structurally: should this account ever be on the board?

ICP fit changes on the scale of quarters and drives list membership. It is deliberately separate from the Intent score, which changes weekly and drives sequencing. The two are held apart and summed at no point: a perfect-fit account in a quiet quarter and a poor-fit account with loud activity land on the same number, and those two need opposite plays.

icp = Σ ( weightinput × scoreinput ) / 100   weights sum to 100, so the score reads as a percentage of achievable fit
ceiling = Σ ( weightinput × [ scoreinput or 100 if unmeasured ] ) / 100

The five inputs

Weights are the one part of the model meant to be edited.

InputWeightHow it scoresWhy it is in the model
eng_band 50 100 at 5 to 50 engineers. 35 under 5, 15 under 2. Decays to 55 at 51 to 100 and 35 above, tagging the row via team so it stays on the board. The buying unit is a team of 5 to 50 with one owner. The floor is about ability to spend, not about who has the pain: a three-engineer team has the review bottleneck in its purest form, and one of the published case-study customers is a reference at roughly that size.
distributed 22 100 with an offshore or cross-timezone staff cluster. 0 when locations were read and the team is co-located. It raises a score and never selects an account, and it is the second heaviest input. The named root cause in one reference customer: juniors blocked waiting for the only reviewer to wake up in another timezone.
data_sensitivity 16 100 when a regime is named and self-attributed. It raises a score and never selects an account. Repeats across four of the seven published case studies, and it is a property of the data itself. An in-progress program is labeled and scored, because an active audit is a dated live trigger.
staff_metro 6 100 in an event metro with 20%+ of staff, 60 in one below 20%, 30 outside the event metros. The weakest input by design, and it must stay weak. This counts staff by metro. LinkedIn publishes engineers by metro separately, and the two are never combined, so identical numbers can mean opposite things. It exists for event serviceability alone.
scm 6 Supported platform confirmed. 100 for a verified GitHub or GitLab org, 75 for a mention in their own requisitions. No source control, no product. A verified org outranks a mention because the org is a checkable fact and the mention is someone's job ad. A private org is not a negative: most companies keep product repos closed, which says nothing about fit.

Missing data scores zero, and carries a ceiling

A deliberate departure from the rule used on the GitHub signals.

An unmeasured input scores zero and flags the row for enrichment, and it stays in the average. The cost is real and worth stating plainly: a zeroed null sorts exactly like a genuine bad fit, so "we skipped the check" and "we looked and it is bad" become indistinguishable in the ranking.

The ceiling is what keeps that honest. It is the score the account would reach if every unmeasured input came back full, so the gap between score and ceiling is the share of the rating that is currently guesswork.

Absent and unmeasured are separate readings

The two are separated per input, because collapsing them was an early defect in this model. A null offshore value inside a staff-location record means the locations were read and the team is co-located: that is a measurement, it scores zero legitimately, and it is not enrichment work. No location record at all is a gap. The same split applies to compliance: requisition bodies read with no regime named is a measurement; no bodies captured is a gap. Conflating them once flagged 80 accounts as needing enrichment on a field that had already been measured.

Tiers, and the rows still being collected

Thresholds come from this model's own distribution, since the two models have different shapes.

A to D split the ranked accounts into four equal groups. Tier U marks a row still being collected. It is what a row gets when fewer than half its inputs are measured, and those rows sit outside the four groups entirely so that a coverage gap can stay out of the ranking.

The evidence rules

Every value carries the sentence it came from, because a keyword match is eyeballed before it is scored.

These rules were not written in advance. Each one exists because a specific false positive would otherwise have scored, and each was caught by reading the matches by hand.

  • Attribution is scoped to the matched sentence and decided by proximity. Whichever subject sits closest to the named regime is the one making the claim. One company's security page lists ISO 27001, PCI and SOC attestations that belong to its hosting provider and says so; another names its cloud vendor's SOC 2 before stating its own. Read over a wide window instead and attribution always returns "self", because some earlier sentence contains the word "our".
  • GDPR is recorded and left out of the score. Nearly every company serving EU users says it somewhere in a privacy policy. It is boilerplate that reads the same across accounts, so the input stays on the regimes a company earns.
  • A platform named on a site is usually not a statement about where that company's code lives. Four distinct ways this goes wrong, all seen in one pass: an integration list (a company integrates with Azure DevOps and keeps its own code on GitHub), a product surface ("you log in with your GitHub, GitLab or Bitbucket account" describes what the product scans), an auth option ("Sign in with Azure DevOps" on a compliance portal), and CSS noise (fa-bitbucket, a Font Awesome icon class). None of them sets the blocked flag.
  • Organizations are bound by domain. An org is accepted only when its stated website resolves to the account's own domain. A GitHub link on a docs page is a lead, not a finding: one company's docs link a third party's org through an analytics dependency.
  • Engineering headcount is measured or left empty. The band's weight is held in the ceiling until the figure is collected.
A crawler defect worth remembering. robots.txt was fetched without an Accept header, thirteen live sites returned 403 for it, and Python's parser turns a 403 into disallow-everything. All thirteen were recorded as "unreachable" when the request had simply been refused at the door. Fixing that one header recovered eight compliance signals. Output that reads like a clean result is the most expensive kind of bug in this pipeline.

ICP A and ICP B

Where the split came from, how it reaches the model, and what is still unresolved.

The two-ICP framing comes from the five recorded founder episodes, where the company describes selling to two populations. The split is theirs and the wording here is mine:

ICP A

Small, AI-native engineering teams

Wedge: raw speed. Output has outrun the people available to review it.

  • Archetype is a three-engineer team plus a CEO who codes, shipping 950+ pull requests a month
  • Fast to close, no procurement, immediate value, strong references
  • Buying is bottom-up: an engineer champion adopts, then spreads it sideways
ICP B

Larger, regulated organizations

Wedge: security, compliance and visibility.

  • Archetypes are the enterprise case-study logos and the regulated fintech references
  • In regulated environments InfoSec must be the first group involved, which is the qualification question
  • Some forbid background or multi-stream agents entirely; everything must be supervised and verified

How the split reaches the model

Each segment has its own model. Model A is the one on this page: it scores the whole 219-account universe on structure and knows nothing about segments, and the ICP A list is its ranking restricted to accounts that pass the agent filter. Model B is a separate scorer with its own five inputs, weighted for governance, over a 104-account regulated universe. The two share one input, data_sensitivity, weighted 16 in Model A and 40 in Model B.

Model A carries the distinction as inputs. Regulated data scores through data_sensitivity and team shape through eng_band, which is also why vertical is absent: seven published customers span seven unrelated verticals, and the only attribute that repeats across them is handling regulated or sensitive data.

Two things to resolve, and both change the model.

1. Regulated or unregulated. Every piece of evidence in the corpus says regulated, and every named example is a regulated organization, so the governance input is defined on that basis.

2. ICP A has nothing of its own that lifts it. The top of Model A's ranking is regulated-data companies with distributed engineering, which is the ICP B shape. On the published ICP A list 13 of the 25 rows score exactly 78, on team size and source control alone with nothing else scoring, so the bottom half of that list carries no meaningful order. For ICP A to rank on its own terms it needs an input that recognizes it: measured velocity, agent adoption, or small team against high output. Today the model can see ICP B and is blind to what makes ICP A distinctive.
