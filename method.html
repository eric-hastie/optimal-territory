The shape of the model

One question, answered structurally: should this account ever be on the board?

ICP fit changes on the scale of quarters and drives list membership. It is deliberately separate from the Intent score, which changes weekly and drives sequencing. The two are never summed: a perfect-fit account in a quiet quarter and a poor-fit account with loud activity land on the same number, and those two need opposite plays.

icp = Σ ( weightterm × scoreterm ) / 100   weights sum to 100, so the score reads as a percentage of achievable fit
ceiling = Σ ( weightterm × [ scoreterm or 100 if unmeasured ] ) / 100

The six terms

Weights are the one part of the model meant to be edited.

TermWeightHow it scoresWhy it is in the model
eng_band 42 100 at 5 to 50 engineers. 35 under 5, 15 under 2. Decays to 55 at 51 to 100 and 35 above, tagging the row via team rather than cutting it. The buying unit is a team of 5 to 50 with one owner. The floor is about ability to spend, not about who has the pain: a three-engineer team has the serialization problem in its purest form, and one of the published case-study customers is a reference at roughly that size.
scm 18 100 for a verified GitHub or GitLab org. 75 for a mention in requisition text with no org resolved. No source control, no product. A verified org outranks a mention because the org is a checkable fact and the mention is someone's job ad. A private org is not a negative: most companies keep product repos closed, which says nothing about fit.
eng_share 15 100 at 25% or more of staff. 70 between 10 and 25%. 35 below 10%. 15 engineers in a 236-person company is a different organisation from 15 in a 30-person company, and the band alone cannot see the difference. Observed quartiles across the board: 5 / 18 / 28 / 43.
data_sensitivity 10 100 when a regime is named and self-attributed. 0 when we looked and found none. An amplifier, never a selector. Repeats across four of the seven published case studies, and it is a property of the data rather than an industry code. An in-progress programme is labelled and scored, because an active audit is a dated live trigger rather than a weaker certificate.
distributed 8 100 with an offshore or cross-timezone staff cluster. 0 when locations were read and the team is co-located. Amplifier. The named root cause in one reference customer: juniors blocked waiting for the only reviewer to wake up in another timezone.
staff_metro 7 100 in an event metro with 20%+ of staff, 60 in one below 20%, 30 outside the event metros. The weakest term by design, and it must stay weak. This is staff by metro, not engineers by metro: the two facets are published separately and never crossed, so identical numbers can mean opposite things. It exists for event serviceability, never as evidence of fit.

How much do the weights actually matter

Less than you would expect, which is the most useful thing the model has said so far. Halving or doubling any single weight moves the average account between one and seven places, and changes the top twenty by at most three accounts.

The lever is coverage, not weighting. Arguing about a weight that moves the average account four places is cheaper than filling in a term that is blank on half the universe, which is why the last pass was enrichment rather than tuning.

Missing data scores zero, and carries a ceiling

A deliberate departure from the rule used on the GitHub signals.

An unmeasured term scores zero and flags the row for enrichment, rather than being dropped from the average. The cost is real and worth stating plainly: a zeroed null sorts exactly like a genuine bad fit, so "we never looked" and "we looked and it is bad" become indistinguishable in the ranking.

The ceiling is what keeps that honest. It is the score the account would reach if every unmeasured term came back full, so the gap between score and ceiling is the share of the rating that is currently guesswork.

Measured

icp 30, ceiling 34

A bad account. Nearly everything was checked and it still scores low. This is a judgment, and you can act on it.

Unmeasured

An unmeasured account wearing a bad score. Nothing here is a finding about the company. It is a finding about our coverage.

Sort on the score. Read the ceiling before concluding anything about a low row.

Absent is not the same as unmeasured

The two are separated per term, because collapsing them was an early defect in this model. A null offshore value inside a staff-location record means the locations were read and the team is co-located: that is a measurement, it scores zero legitimately, and it is not enrichment work. No location record at all is a gap. The same split applies to compliance: requisition bodies read with no regime named is a measurement; no bodies captured is a gap. Conflating them once flagged 80 accounts as needing enrichment on a field that had already been measured.

Tiers, and the accounts that do not get one

Thresholds come from this model's own distribution and are never copied from the Intent model.

A to D are quartiles cut across the ranked population. Tier U is not a bad tier. It is what a row gets when fewer than half its terms are measured, and those rows sit outside the quartile cut entirely so that a coverage gap can never wear a ranking.

The 110 unranked accounts are one flat block, not a long tail. They exist only in the firmographics universe: a domain and a total headcount and nothing else. No engineering headcount, no staff locations, which is 72 of the 100 available points. Every one of them lands at 2 of 6 terms and scores at most 28 against a median ceiling of 100. The unblock is the LinkedIn people-facet pass or Sales Navigator, and no amount of site crawling substitutes for it.

A degeneracy check runs on every build: verify.py flags any field where more than 55% of accounts share one value. The most common single score currently covers 27%.

Azure DevOps and Bitbucket do not zero the score

They set BLOCKED and the account ranks in its own group. Both platforms are listed as in active development, so these are pre-qualified accounts waiting on a release date rather than dead ends. Exactly one account across 219 currently carries the flag, and that is certainly an undercount given how little requisition body text exists.

The evidence rules

Every value carries the sentence it came from, because a keyword match is eyeballed before it is scored.

These rules were not written in advance. Each one exists because a specific false positive would otherwise have scored, and each was caught by reading the matches rather than by any automated check.

  • Attribution is scoped to the matched sentence and decided by proximity. Whichever subject sits closest to the named regime is the one making the claim. One company's security page lists ISO 27001, PCI and SOC attestations that belong to its hosting provider and says so; another names its cloud vendor's SOC 2 before stating its own. Read over a wide window instead and attribution always returns "self", because some earlier sentence contains the word "our".
  • GDPR is recorded and never scored. Nearly every company serving EU users says it somewhere in a privacy policy. It is boilerplate, it does not discriminate between accounts, and scoring it would push the term toward the degenerate state the verifier exists to catch.
  • A platform named on a site is usually not a statement about where that company's code lives. Four distinct ways this goes wrong, all seen in one pass: an integration list (a company integrates with Azure DevOps and keeps its own code on GitHub), a product surface ("you log in with your GitHub, GitLab or Bitbucket account" describes what the product scans), an auth option ("Sign in with Azure DevOps" on a compliance portal), and CSS noise (fa-bitbucket, a Font Awesome icon class). None of them sets the blocked flag.
  • Organisations are bound by domain, never by name. An org is accepted only when its stated website resolves to the account's own domain. A GitHub link on a docs page is a lead, not a finding: one company's docs link a third party's org through an analytics dependency.
  • Engineering headcount is never estimated. Not from total headcount, not from requisition counts, not from any heuristic. The one permitted derivation is a bound: when engineering is absent from LinkedIn's top-five functions it must sit below the fifth-largest count, so that count becomes a ceiling. Those rows are flagged bounded and never presented as measured.
A crawler defect worth remembering. robots.txt was fetched without an Accept header, thirteen live sites returned 403 for it, and Python's parser turns a 403 into disallow-everything. All thirteen were recorded as "unreachable" when they had never actually been asked. Fixing that one header recovered eight compliance signals. Output that reads like a clean result is the most expensive kind of bug in this pipeline.

ICP A and ICP B

Where the split came from, what happened to it, and what is still unresolved.

The two-ICP framing comes from the five recorded founder episodes, where the company describes selling to two populations rather than one. It is their own words, not an inference:

ICP A

Small, AI-native, green-field teams with runaway velocity

Wedge: raw speed. Output has outrun the people available to review it.

  • Archetype is a three-engineer team plus a CEO who codes, shipping 950+ pull requests a month
  • Fast to close, no procurement, immediate value, strong references
  • Buying is bottom-up: an engineer champion adopts, then spreads it sideways
ICP B

Larger, regulated organisations

Wedge: security, compliance and visibility rather than speed.

  • Archetypes are the enterprise case-study logos and the regulated fintech references
  • In regulated environments InfoSec must be the first group involved, which is the qualification question
  • Some forbid background or multi-stream agents entirely; everything must be supervised and verified
  • The large logos each bought for one team, not the company

What actually happened to the split

The original plan was two boards of 25, each with its own scoring model. That is not what was built, and the plan was superseded rather than abandoned. The universe was assembled as 115 accounts segmented by metro instead, because the dinner series is the pipeline engine and each metro view doubles as a guest list. The living ICP definition is now a single ICP with a Bay Area sub-segment carve-out, and it states explicitly that this is not a vertical play: seven published customers span seven unrelated verticals, and the only attribute that repeats is handling regulated or sensitive data.

So the A/B distinction survives in the model as terms rather than as separate boards. Regulated data is the data_sensitivity amplifier. Team shape is eng_band and eng_share. Nothing in the current scorer knows which ICP an account belongs to.

Two things to resolve, and both change the model.

1. Regulated or unregulated. Every piece of evidence in the corpus says regulated, and every named example is a regulated organisation, so the governance amplifier is defined on that basis.

2. ICP A has no amplifier of its own. The top of the current ranking is regulated-data companies with distributed engineering, which is ICP B. Every account from roughly rank 19 downward bunches at the same score on team size and source control alone, with no amplifier firing at all. If ICP A is meant to compete for the top of the board, it needs a term that recognises it: measured velocity, or agent adoption, or small-team-high-output. Today the model can see ICP B and is blind to what makes ICP A distinctive.
